Falls Church is considering whether to bring back Little City Sips after a June pilot drew more than 4,300 attendees and reportedly increased sales at participating restaurants.

The two-and-a-half-hour event, held June 13 in downtown Falls Church, temporarily allowed adults age 21 and older to carry alcoholic beverages outdoors in official branded cups. Drinks had to be purchased from participating businesses and remain within the designated event area.

The pilot represented a temporary change in how Falls Church handles open alcoholic containers in public spaces. City officials were evaluating crowd conditions, safety and participant feedback as they considered whether Little City Sips could become a seasonal event.

Nine restaurants and venues participated, including Ireland’s Four Provinces, Pho 88 and Stratford Garden. Six live bands and other entertainment were spread among downtown gathering spaces.

Businesses used color-coded window signs to tell visitors where drinks could be purchased and carried. Green signs reading “Sold Here” identified establishments selling alcoholic beverages in the branded cups. Blue “Welcome” signs marked businesses that allowed customers to enter while carrying those drinks. Audacious Aleworks, for example, encouraged attendees to come inside, sit down, drink beer and watch sports.

Attendance peaked at approximately 2,800 people around 8 p.m., according to figures presented in the sole supplied report. The same report placed total attendance above 4,300, but it did not explain how the crowd was counted or identify an underlying dataset. No independent source was supplied to corroborate either figure.

Participating restaurants reportedly recorded an approximately 20 percent increase in meal sales and a 12 percent increase in beer sales. Those figures also come from the sole report, which did not identify the comparison period, calculation method or business-level data behind the percentages. The numbers therefore indicate the gains described to the Economic Development Authority but do not support a broader conclusion about the pilot’s overall economic effect.

The event’s precise boundaries are also unclear in the supplied account. One description places the designated area within North Washington Street, West Broad Street, Maple Street and Park Avenue. Elsewhere, the account refers more narrowly to boundaries along West Broad Street and Park Avenue. The narrower wording may be shorthand, but the available information does not resolve the difference.

Economic Development Authority members discussed holding another Little City Sips and potentially expanding the designated area. Ross Litkenhous, the authority’s chair, described the pilot favorably while emphasizing the crowd size that worked for the initial event.

“It was the perfect amount of people and had a very positive outcome,” Litkenhous said.

That assessment was not presented as unanimous support for unrestricted growth. Some participants in the discussion worried that expanding too quickly could make the event unruly and diminish the qualities that made the pilot appealing. Their concern puts crowd management alongside turnout and restaurant sales as the city evaluates whether and how the event should return.

Mayor Letty Hardi attended the authority meeting and expressed enthusiasm about both the pilot and the possibility of future events. The discussion nevertheless left city officials with several factors to assess: the reported demand, participating businesses’ sales results, the boundaries of the event area and whether future crowds could be accommodated safely.

For Falls Church, the central question is whether Little City Sips can retain the scale praised after its debut while becoming a recurring downtown event. The Economic Development Authority’s discussion of another installment and a possible expansion shows interest in continuing the concept, while the concerns raised about rapid growth underscore the need for a measured approach.
