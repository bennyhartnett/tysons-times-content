Federal workforce cuts are putting growing pressure on Fairfax County, where many households depend on government agencies and the contractors that support them. The impact could spread well beyond laid-off workers as offices empty, commuters stay home and nearby businesses lose customers.

The Virginia Employment Commission has created a resource section for former federal employees and others affected by the government transition. It directs workers toward several forms of assistance, but navigating the options can be complicated.

Mount Vernon resident Jim Weinstein, an employment specialist who operates an assistance office from his Hollin Hills home, described the job market as “horrifying.”

“People are hoping things aren’t that bad,” Weinstein said, adding that such optimism may not be realistic. Despite the number of openings displayed on Monster, Indeed and similar websites, he said many employers are not hiring large numbers of people.

“There’s no jobs out there,” he said.

Weinstein said improving a résumé can help, but networking may be the most effective strategy. Volunteering could also give displaced workers a way to make professional connections.

The competition is already intense. Weinstein cited a former USAID worker who attended a Fairfax County Public Schools job fair and found hundreds of other applicants seeking work.

“It’s very over-saturated now,” he said.

Braddock District Supervisor James R. Walkinshaw addressed the federal workforce changes in his newsletter, The Walkinshaw Advisory. He criticized the Trump administration’s approach and encouraged residents to contact Fairfax County’s congressional delegation, noting that county officials have limited power to intervene.

On Monday, Feb. 24, Gov. Glenn Youngkin launched the “Virginia Has Jobs” initiative and outlined a Federal Worker Support Resource Package for Virginians affected by changes to the federal workforce.

The initiative highlighted 250,000 open positions across Virginia. Northern Virginia listings included private-sector opportunities with Fortune 500 companies such as CACI and Booz Allen Hamilton. Public-sector listings included jobs with Fairfax, Arlington and Alexandria, along with several school systems.

Fairfax County Public Schools recently held a job fair aimed at recruiting former federal employees for service and administrative positions.

Superintendent Michelle Reid said federal workers could bring a strong commitment to public service.

“We want people that have that service-above-self mentality and we know our Federal workers have that,” Reid said.

The economic consequences could extend throughout the region. When federal agencies shed jobs, contractors may also reduce their workforces. Offices can become vacant, echoing conditions seen during the pandemic, while delis and other nearby businesses lose customers.

Fewer commuters and more cautious household spending could further reduce the amount of money circulating through the local economy.

“If you get laid off you aren’t going to take your family out to a fancy dinner,” Weinstein said.

The Virginia Employment Commission launched a redesigned website in late January featuring streamlined navigation, step-by-step guidance, easier access to frequently asked questions and real-time responses. Job openings are also available through the government and public school career pages for Fairfax County, Arlington County and the City of Alexandria.
