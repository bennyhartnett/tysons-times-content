Stress does not always start with us. In a volatile, uncertain, complex and ambiguous world, anxiety, anger and exhaustion can move rapidly through workplaces, social circles, media feeds and online platforms.

That spread reflects a basic human survival system. Emotions help the brain interpret situations, select responses and learn from experience. Fear and anger prepare the body to confront or escape danger, while joy and affection encourage cooperation, connection and belonging.

Humans also tend to mirror the emotions of others. For early communities, that emotional contagion offered practical advantages: Groups could react to threats before every person understood the danger, communicate without words and strengthen the trust needed for teamwork and caregiving.

Modern life exposes that ancient system to an extraordinary volume of emotional signals. Face-to-face encounters, social networks, news coverage and remote-work platforms can all transmit feelings. In stressful environments, fear, frustration, sadness and anxiety may spread as readily as positive emotions.

The effects can accumulate. Shared distress can undermine psychological safety and resilience, reduce hope, fuel cynicism and interfere with clear thinking and sound decisions. At its worst, the cycle contributes to emotional exhaustion and collective burnout.

Several practices can help interrupt it.

Start by developing greater self-awareness. Notice how pressure changes your thoughts, emotions, physical reactions and behavior. Strengths can become liabilities under stress, affecting how you interpret events, judge other people and present yourself.

Pay attention to whose emotions influence you most. Certain people may have a stronger effect on your mood, thinking and actions than others. Recognizing those patterns can help you respond intentionally instead of absorbing them automatically.

Set boundaries around both media and personal interactions. Decide what information you will consume, how often you will engage with it and when you need distance. Limits are not necessarily about rejecting others; they can protect the energy and concentration required to act according to your needs and values.

Focus on what remains within your control. You may not be able to change an event or another person’s behavior, but you can direct your attention, adjust your attitude and choose your next step. Look for practical actions and lessons instead of trying to control every variable.

Mindfulness can also bring attention back to the present. One simple exercise is to identify the first thing you notice—a sight, sound, smell or other sensation—and continue naming details around you until your attention feels grounded again.

Finally, challenge the mental narrative intensifying your reaction. Ask what you are feeling, what happened and what story you are telling yourself about it. Consider whether that story is accurate or useful, whether another interpretation is possible and what is most likely true. Then identify what you can control and act on it.
