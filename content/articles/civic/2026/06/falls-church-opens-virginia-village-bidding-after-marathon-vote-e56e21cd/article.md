After a five-and-a-half-hour meeting that stretched to 1:11 a.m. Tuesday, July 23, the Falls Church City Council voted 6-1 to seek development proposals for Virginia Village, advancing a closely watched affordable-housing project while requiring public summaries of the competing plans.

Virginia Village covers 4.5 acres along South Maple Avenue between Bowl America and the Tax Analysts building. The city says the neighborhood contains some of Falls Church’s few market-rate affordable homes: 20 four-unit apartment buildings dating to the 1940s.

Over the past decade, Falls Church has strategically purchased nine Virginia Village properties with the goal of preserving and expanding the neighborhood’s affordable housing.

The newly authorized request for proposals, or RFP, will invite developers to show what could be built or rehabilitated on the city-owned land.

## Public review built into proposal process

The Virginia Public Procurement Act prevents the city from releasing complete proposals containing developers’ proprietary information before a contract is awarded. To provide a measure of transparency, the Council will require each bidder to submit a comprehensive executive summary for public review.

Those summaries are expected to include basic visual plans and massing models showing the general volume, scale and shape of each proposal. The city plans to release them in early August, giving residents four to five weeks to evaluate the concepts and submit comments.

An evaluation committee is expected to bring its final recommendations to the Council in September 2026.

The review process is intended to address a central concern voiced repeatedly by residents: whether the public will have a meaningful opportunity to assess the plans before the city chooses a preferred developer.

## Three dramatically different paths

The RFP allows developers to respond with concepts based on three broad redevelopment scenarios.

The first would preserve the existing footprint of the city-owned quadplexes while substantially modernizing them. Limited additions, such as another floor or small extensions, could create more apartments without completely replacing the community.

The second option would demolish the 1940s buildings and replace them with new, relatively low-density apartment communities of up to four stories.

The most intensive alternative would allow a larger demolition-and-rebuilding project, with buildings between five and seven stories in selected areas. That approach could produce substantially more affordable housing but has generated the strongest concerns about height, scale and compatibility with nearby Winter Hill and Big Chimneys Park.

A hybrid proposal combining elements of those approaches could also emerge.

Before redevelopment proceeds, the Planning Office is revisiting the 2013 South Washington Street Small Area Plan and the 2014 Downtown Small Area Plan. Officials want to ensure their recommendations for Virginia Village reflect current community priorities, recent updates to the Comprehensive Plan and other city policies.

## Housing demand drives urgency

Falls Church has an affordable-housing waiting list of more than 700 people, according to figures cited by Mayor Letty Hardi and other participants. Several speakers placed the number of applicants above 900, highlighting both the scale of demand and disagreement over the precise count.

Hardi has argued that rising land and construction costs make excessive delay risky. In a June 19 constituent newsletter, she described the RFP as the beginning of a process likely to continue into 2027, with numerous decisions still ahead.

The anticipated steps include public presentations, committee scoring of proposals, selection of a development partner, negotiations over interim and comprehensive agreements, final zoning action, site-plan review in early 2027 and an affordable-housing funding application to Virginia Housing in March 2027.

Hardi said concerns about setbacks, building-height transitions and neighborhood compatibility warrant thoughtful answers. At the same time, she warned that restrictions making a project financially unworkable could result in fewer affordable homes, more expensive housing or no project at all.

## Residents split over timing and scale

Since March, the city has conducted meetings and neighborhood outreach to shape the RFP. Public testimony during the June 22-23 Council meeting showed broad support for affordable housing but sharp disagreement over how quickly the city should proceed and how much development the site should carry.

No speaker openly opposed the city’s goal of adding affordable homes. The divide centered instead on whether the RFP preserves sufficient public control, whether zoning should be settled first and whether buildings of up to seven stories belong beside Winter Hill.

Sonya Breehey, Northern Virginia advocacy director for the Coalition for Smarter Growth and a Falls Church resident, supported the city’s approach. She described Virginia Village as a walkable location served by bus routes and within a short bus or bicycle trip of two Metro stations.

Breehey praised provisions seeking apartments for larger households, modern and sustainable buildings, public open space, shade trees and pedestrian access to Big Chimneys Park. She also supported offering homes to households earning between 30 and 80 percent of area median income, with 30 percent of the affordable units targeted to households at 58 percent of AMI or below.

Virginia Village tenant Maria Belen Quellet also urged the city to move ahead. She said residents had invested months in meetings and that staff had listened to their concerns. In her view, issuing the RFP would not decide the neighborhood’s future; it would reveal what options are actually possible.

Other supporters, including Alex Maricola, Robin Remy and Alex Jasura, argued that the consequences of delaying affordable housing should be weighed against concerns about height and neighborhood change. Remy said affordable housing had made it possible for her to live in Falls Church and urged officials to place disputes over setbacks and one-story differences in the context of hundreds of households waiting for homes.

Joseph Schiarizzi called the RFP more transparent than other development processes because residents would see actual concepts before final zoning approval. He encouraged the city to consider a municipal bond and an even larger project, while also making future housing accessible to people with disabilities. He noted that the existing Virginia Village buildings lack wheelchair ramps.

## Calls to slow down

Several Winter Hill residents supported affordable housing but asked the Council to delay the RFP, establish zoning rules first or remove the largest redevelopment option.

Joseph Carmody warned that requesting binding proposals too early could leave the Council and community facing a take-it-or-leave-it choice. He recommended completing an initial zoning review, publishing a draft solicitation for public and developer feedback, and then finalizing the competitive RFP.

Laura Gogal, an attorney with professional experience handling requests for proposals, voiced a similar concern. Details omitted from an RFP can be difficult to negotiate after proposals arrive, she said, citing matters such as setbacks and accessible routes.

Rohini Winters suggested replacing the RFP with a request for information. That would allow the city to gather industry ideas about the smaller redevelopment scenarios without moving immediately into a formal competition.

Teresa Clark, a Winter Hill resident of 35 years, supported affordable housing but favored a three-story building with substantial setbacks and outdoor space. Mary Lou Pickel advocated for three- or four-story development comparable in scale to Winter Hill, arguing that six- or seven-story construction would overwhelm nearby homes and the park.

Kathy Kleinman likewise urged the city to retain the rehabilitation and low-intensity options while eliminating the five- to seven-story alternative. She said affordable housing should blend into the existing community instead of being visually separated in one large building.

Other residents expressed distrust based on previous Falls Church projects. Carl Anderson, who has lived in Winter Hill for 48 years, pointed to the loss of buffers and height concessions in earlier developments. He also recalled structural damage to several homes during excavation and blasting for Pearson Square in the early 2000s.

Heather Pressler said residents need time to discuss what the new development would look and feel like before a proposal is selected. She also asked the city to examine affordable homeownership, noting that some former Winter Hill residents left because they could not purchase property.

## Private owners and preservation questions

Virginia Village includes privately owned quadplexes as well as the properties acquired by the city.

Beverly Warnock spoke for herself and her father, who has owned and lived in an independent Virginia Village quadplex for 60 years. She said private owners had been assured the city would not act on their land, but she remained concerned that zoning changes could limit property rights or reduce the value and flexibility of family investments.

Rebecca Johnson asked the city to obtain a formal historic survey through the Virginia Department of Historic Resources before approving zoning changes. Virginia Village was built during the World War II expansion of housing for war workers, she said, and a historic designation could make restoration tax credits available to city and private owners.

Johnson promoted a “blended preservation” approach that would add homes and increase density while maintaining the neighborhood’s architectural scale. She argued that preservation could reduce displacement and protect nearby Big Chimneys Park and the historic E.B. Henderson site.

## Debate over whom the housing should serve

Former Council member and vice mayor Hal Lippman urged Falls Church to direct more of the project toward residents with the lowest incomes.

Since 2006, he said, the city has added 204 affordable homes beyond previously existing units. Of those, 189—or 88 percent—serve households earning between 60 and 80 percent of area median income.

With AMI now at $164,000, a family of four at the 60-percent level earns nearly $100,000, Lippman said. By comparison, only 3 percent of city rentals serve households at 40 percent of AMI, currently about $65,000 for four people. Roughly one-third of applicants on the affordable-housing waiting list earn 30 percent of AMI or less, or just under $50,000 for a family of four.

He asked the Council to lower the income thresholds in the proposal and focus more directly on workers earning between roughly $30,000 and $50,000 annually. He also connected the housing debate to Falls Church’s long-running struggle to achieve greater racial and economic diversity.

## Traffic, access and neighborhood impacts

Residents also raised practical questions that could shape future plans.

Stephanie Powell, a Cherry Hill townhouse owner on Gundry Drive, asked the city to relocate accessible entry to Big Chimneys Park away from her community’s private parking lot. She also proposed connecting Shirley Street to Maple Avenue near the Tax Analysts property, creating a traffic loop instead of leaving the development with a single way in and out.

Nevin Jaffer supported affordable housing but argued that it should be distributed throughout Falls Church rather than concentrated in one location. Jaffer worried that a large vertical building could reduce sunlight, change Winter Hill’s character and affect nearby property values.

Supporters countered that city studies found proposed buildings would cast little shadow into Winter Hill or Big Chimneys Park. They asked the Council to judge those concerns against the urgent need for affordable homes.

With the RFP now authorized, that debate will move from general possibilities to actual development concepts. Residents will get their next major opportunity to influence the outcome when the executive summaries and visual plans are released for public review.
