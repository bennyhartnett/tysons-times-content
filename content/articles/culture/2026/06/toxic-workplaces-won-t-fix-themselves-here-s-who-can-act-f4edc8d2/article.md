Abusive remarks, quiet power struggles and arbitrary rules can linger in a worker’s memory long after the job ends. Toxic workplaces also carry broader costs: employees leave, health suffers, burnout rises, productivity slips and fewer people feel safe enough to speak up.

There is no single, universally accepted definition of a toxic workplace. A damaging environment, however, often reveals itself through persistent patterns rather than one isolated conflict.

Warning signs can include bullying, harassment, intimidation and disrespect; favoritism or fear-based leadership; excessive control or neglect; low psychological safety; and unreasonable deadlines that repeatedly push employees beyond their limits.

What someone can do about those conditions depends largely on how much power they hold within the organization.

## Employees Can Protect Themselves and Prepare to Leave

Employees may have limited influence over workplace culture, but they can take steps to protect their health and preserve their options.

Healthy coping matters. Reading, exercising and getting enough sleep can provide more support than excessive drinking, substance use, binge-watching or endless scrolling. Setting boundaries and staying clear of workplace drama can also reduce exposure to unnecessary stress.

Workers can document troubling conduct and, if they are uncomfortable approaching human resources, consider seeking guidance from an employee-focused labor and employment attorney.

Trusted friends and relatives can help workers understand what is happening, recognize that harmful behavior is not normal and develop an exit strategy. A therapist may also provide support when workplace stress becomes overwhelming or traumatic.

Leaving immediately may not be financially possible, but preparation can begin at once. Workers can update their résumés, contact people in their fields and continue building professional skills while searching for another opportunity.

## Managers Can Shield Their Teams

Mid-level managers occupy a difficult position, facing demands from senior leaders while absorbing distress from employees below them. They may not have enough authority to repair the entire organization, but they can either soften the effects of a toxic culture or make them worse.

Managers who want to protect their teams must first care for themselves. Unprocessed pressure can easily spill onto employees, even when that is not the manager’s intention.

They can also recognize individual and team achievements, model healthy boundaries, admit mistakes, ask for help and filter unnecessary noise from above. When senior leaders react with panic, managers can translate that anxiety into clear, achievable priorities.

Creating space for candid discussion without retaliation is especially important. Employees are more likely to identify problems and ethical risks when they believe it is safe to speak.

## Senior Leaders Hold the Greatest Responsibility

Everyone contributes to workplace culture, but senior executives have the most power to change it. A turnaround ultimately depends on whether they are willing to acknowledge the problem and act.

Some leaders dismiss toxic behavior as the work of a few difficult personalities or describe it as the price of a high-performance culture. Others frame the damage as an employee resilience problem or introduce wellness benefits and superficial culture initiatives without addressing harmful job conditions.

Acknowledging toxicity can threaten leaders’ reputations and expose failures in the systems they oversee. Yet refusing to confront it can produce even greater costs through turnover, lost productivity, health expenses, reputational damage and potential legal risk.

Outside consultants may help leaders identify root causes that are difficult to see from inside the organization. Although that assistance carries a cost, continued inaction may prove far more expensive.

No toxic workplace repairs itself. Employees can protect themselves and plan an exit, managers can reduce the harm reaching their teams, and executives can confront the organizational conditions driving the problem. At every level, recognizing toxicity is only the beginning; improvement requires action.
