Mountain View High School’s winter graduates celebrated more than earning diplomas on Thursday, Feb. 13. They marked the end of journeys shaped by demanding jobs, language barriers and personal struggles that often made finishing school seem out of reach.

Erin Whatley, in her first year as Mountain View’s principal, told the Class of 2025 that their achievement also belonged to the teachers, relatives and friends who helped them through their most difficult days.

The students’ perseverance, she said, proved those challenges had been worth confronting.

Mountain View primarily serves students who have faced significant obstacles. Some arrived from other countries without speaking English. Others balanced classes with full-time work or serious personal responsibilities.

Whatley said the graduates had expanded the meaning of success by choosing an educational path suited to their lives instead of following expectations. Their experiences, she told them, demonstrated resilience that could propel them through college, employment, entrepreneurship or whatever they pursue next.

She encouraged the seniors to approach their futures with the same determination that carried them to graduation. Success, she said, extends beyond a diploma and includes the courage to continue moving forward while recognizing one’s own potential.

Whatley also pointed to the need for a new generation of independent and innovative thinkers as the world changes. She congratulated the students for doing more than taking an unconventional route, saying they had created paths of their own.

Guest speaker Geovanny Ponce, Fairfax County Public Schools’ chief of schools, followed with a story that reflected many graduates’ experiences.

Ponce said he was born into poverty in Tegucigalpa, Honduras, and grew up without a father, shoes or reliable access to food. He moved to the United States in 1998 carrying only a backpack and struggled to learn English.

Twenty-seven years later, he stood before the graduates as Dr. Ponce. He recalled arriving as a young immigrant trying to understand an unfamiliar country and said he now feels new again as he works to support FCPS families and the broader community.

Ponce also described facing discrimination because of his accent. He said people sometimes assumed that difficulty speaking English meant he lacked an education.

Despite those experiences, he told the graduates they were prepared for greatness and had received an excellent education from a dedicated Mountain View faculty. He said their hard work reminded him of his own effort to overcome setbacks and continue pursuing an education.

When Ponce was in fourth grade, he said, a teacher suggested that he might stop attending school. Unlike the United States, he explained, his home country did not legally require families to send their children to school.

Seeing young immigrants among the graduates made the moment emotional for him. He urged them to keep searching for opportunities instead of concentrating on obstacles, saying education had opened doors in his life and could do the same for them.

Graduation is a beginning rather than an ending, Ponce said. Whether the students enter college, begin careers, enlist in the military or explore other possibilities, he told them their education and the challenges they had already overcome prepared them for what comes next.

He closed by encouraging the graduates to believe in themselves, remain focused and help others along the way.

“Never forget that your dreams are worth fighting for,” Ponce told them.
