A packed town hall at Yorktown High School became a tense display of anxiety and solidarity as Arlington residents confronted threats to federal jobs, attacks on diversity programs and concerns about rising energy costs.

Some attendees waiting to question Virginia Del. Patrick Hope asked that their names not be published. Others worried about photographs identifying speakers employed by the federal government. Residents spoke of oligarchic influence in Washington and Arlington, while frequent applause reflected a growing determination to act together.

Marguarite Gooden of Hall’s Hill delivered the meeting’s defining message. Noting that few people in the room looked like her, Gooden said she and several friends attended because Black Arlingtonians had hard-earned experience defending their rights.

Gooden argued that attacks on diversity, equity and inclusion programs were discriminatory and urged residents to engage young people, mobilize nonvoters and resist actions coming from Washington.

She pointed to Arlington’s history, including the Black students from her neighborhood who helped begin the desegregation of Virginia schools and the county’s early Black firefighters. Black residents, she said, were accustomed to fighting for their rights, but the current moment required a broader alliance.

“It’s going to take all of us to take our government back,” Gooden told the crowd, drawing a lengthy standing ovation.

Hope applauded before joking, “Marguarite, I just hope you don’t ever run against me!”

Energy auditor Mike Ingram challenged Hope over Dominion Energy and the cost of connecting residential solar installations to the power grid. The 75-year-old, whose community involvement traces back to Arlingtonians for a Better County, said the additional expense discourages solar adoption and undermines efforts to reduce energy costs.

Ingram also raised concerns that households could be forced to absorb higher electricity costs associated with data centers. He asked what Democrats in Richmond—and Hope personally—were doing to confront Dominion.

Hope, who stopped accepting Dominion campaign contributions in 2019, said the State Corporation Commission was examining the matter. Ingram later said the response did not fully answer his question, especially because Virginia lawmakers select the commission’s members and Dominion has long made strategic campaign donations.

Federal employment cuts were another major concern. A longtime Arlington resident affiliated with the National Association of Active and Retired Federal Employees described the hostility toward government workers as startling.

The resident recalled hearing a caller on a radio program celebrate federal layoffs and say government employees would finally learn what losing a job felt like. Given Arlington’s large federal workforce, the NARFE member urged affected residents to seek guidance from the organization’s Federal Benefits Institute.

Throughout the meeting, speakers repeatedly encouraged neighbors to resist passivity and seek available help. Despite claims that the traditional “Arlington Way” has disappeared, the crowd’s response suggested that its emphasis on community action may still endure.
