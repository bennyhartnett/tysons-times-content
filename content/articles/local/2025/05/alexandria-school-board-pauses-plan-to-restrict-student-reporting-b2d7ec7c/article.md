Alexandria school officials have paused consideration of a policy that could give administrators greater power to stop student investigative reporting, following mounting opposition from student journalists and press-freedom advocates.

The School Board Governance Committee halted the process last week but left open the possibility of returning to it later. The pause came after students packed a School Board meeting to challenge the proposal and criticize what they see as a broader lack of transparency within Alexandria City Public Schools.

“It shows the pressure the community has put on the School Board is starting to kick in,” said James Libresco, co-editor of Alexandria City High School’s student publication, Theogony. “They’re realizing this isn’t such a good idea.”

School Board Chairwoman Michelle Rief and Superintendent Melanie Kay-Wyatt declined multiple interview requests about the policy debate. None of the board’s nine elected members agreed to speak on the record.

In a written statement, Rief said the board dais was the “appropriate and best place” for members to deliberate publicly and transparently.

Critics say the reluctance to answer questions reflects a long-standing communications problem. The school division has frequently responded to inquiries with written statements rather than interviews, including on subjects such as demographic changes and transgender policy.

Councilman Abdel-Rahman Elnoubi, who served on the School Board before his election to the City Council last year, said members owe the community an explanation of their positions.

“I’ve always witnessed peer pressure against talking to the media,” Elnoubi said, adding that public comments from board members about controversial issues were sometimes portrayed as unsupportive of the superintendent or staff.

## Proposed Rules Alarm Student Journalists

A document circulated several weeks ago outlined a process that could allow school officials to stop investigative articles before publication. It also raised the prospect of revoking students’ “journalistic privileges” if they published work in established newspapers or new media outlets.

The committee’s pause has left unresolved where individual School Board members stand on protections for student journalists.

Megan Rhyne, executive director of the Virginia Coalition for Open Government, said an insistence on communicating through a single, positive institutional voice can erode public confidence.

“When you refuse to talk to the media or the public,” Rhyne said, people become less willing to give officials the benefit of the doubt when problems arise.

The policy debate followed a series of Theogony investigations into the city’s “high-school project,” which spread Alexandria’s large high school population across four campuses. The students reported that employees were required to reapply for jobs during a reorganization and that late buses sometimes led students to walk between campuses.

Kay-Wyatt declined repeated interview requests from Theogony during that reporting.

Valerie Kibler, president of the Journalism Education Association, said schools should help students learn how to report responsibly on sensitive issues instead of suppressing their work.

“If they are interviewing all of the sides and doing it the right way, the School Board shouldn’t have any problem with that,” Kibler said.

## Who Gets the Final Say?

School officials say the existing student-publications policy is outdated and scheduled for revision. It already allows the principal to block stories, although recent practice has involved administrators reviewing potentially controversial work in advance without stopping publication.

At a public hearing earlier this month, former Theogony faculty adviser Mark Eaton described an arrangement under which he alerted school officials to sensitive reporting before publication. Eaton said that during his 13 years advising the publication, no principal ever asked him to halt an article.

Jonathan Gaston-Falk, an education law attorney with the Student Press Law Center, said that history may have created a reasonable expectation among students that they will continue making editorial decisions even if the written policy says otherwise.

The central dispute is over who should have final authority to stop an article. Governance Committee member Tim Beaty has favored giving that power to the principal. Committee member Abdulahi Abdalla has said the faculty adviser should make the final decision.

Committee Chairwoman Ashley Simpson Baird has sought a middle ground that preserves adult guidance without stripping students of control.

“I just want people to talk to each other,” Baird said at last week’s committee meeting. She suggested finding an approach in which adults facilitate the educational process without such a “heavy hand” that students lose agency.

Press-freedom advocates welcomed the pause but remain concerned that another restrictive proposal could surface in the coming months.

Ian Kalish, an instructor at the University of Virginia First Amendment Clinic, said the fact that a school might have constitutional room to regulate student journalism does not mean it should exercise that authority.

“Student journalists should be allowed to investigate stories, run with stories as they see fit and participate in the full journalistic process without interference from the school,” Kalish said.

## Students Offer Their Own Policy

The student journalists have proposed an alternative framework called “Voices Unbound.” It would explicitly protect students’ First Amendment rights instead of preserving administrators’ power to censor their reporting.

The proposal has received support from the Student Press Law Center, National Scholastic Press Association and Journalism Education Association.

Libresco said students worked with experts to create a policy that has drawn support from thousands of community members. But he said no School Board member has committed to adopting the Voices Unbound framework.
