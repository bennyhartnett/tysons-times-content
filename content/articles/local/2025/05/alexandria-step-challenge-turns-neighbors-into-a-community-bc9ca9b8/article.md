A casual step-count comparison among a few residents has grown into a neighborhood-wide ritual in Alexandria’s Beverley Hills community, bringing more than 50 people together through daily walks.

The 10,000 Steps Neighborhood Challenge began last spring after several neighbors became curious about how much they were moving. Participation spread largely by word of mouth, according to Debbie Tran, 42, who helps manage a neighborhood email list.

“It really began as a lighthearted thing,” Tran said. “A few of us got curious about how much we were moving and decided to compare step counts. Then more people joined in—and now it’s a whole thing.”

Residents use fitness watches, pedometers and mechanical click counters to track their steps. They share updates through a Google Sheet, trade walking suggestions in group texts and leave handwritten encouragement on porches and mailboxes.

Despite its name, the challenge does not require anyone to walk 10,000 steps a day. Some participants aim for half that number, while others exceed it. There is no leaderboard or formal competition; the focus is on staying active and connecting with neighbors.

For retired history teacher Eddie Novak, 67, regular sunrise walks have added structure to his week.

“I walk the same loop every Tuesday and Thursday at sunrise,” Novak said. “At first it was for my health. But then I started looking forward to waving at the same people each week. It gives the day some rhythm.”

Participants range from middle school students training for track to grandparents recovering from knee surgery. The challenge has also sparked sidewalk chalk displays, walks before dinner and a monthly “loop and latte” gathering at a local café.

Maria Ortiz joined after moving to the neighborhood last summer. She said walking helped her learn the area and meet people.

“I didn’t know anyone when I got here,” Ortiz said. “But walking gave me a reason to explore, and now I bump into people I actually recognize. I feel like I belong.”

Neighbors have added their own touches to the experience. One family carries a portable speaker playing mellow music, while another posts changing trivia questions on a chalkboard outside its fence. A resident also designed a shaded, low-traffic “quiet route,” now displayed on a laminated map outside Tran’s front gate.

Some walkers use their outings to notice details beyond their step totals. One records blooming flowers on her phone, while another writes nightly observations about friendly faces, rustling trees and cooking aromas drifting from nearby homes.

“It’s funny how something as simple as walking has made this neighborhood feel more alive,” Tran said. “You’re not just passing people anymore. You’re part of something shared.”

Participants rarely discuss who has logged the most steps. Instead, the challenge’s lasting rewards have been new conversations, familiar faces and a stronger sense of belonging.
