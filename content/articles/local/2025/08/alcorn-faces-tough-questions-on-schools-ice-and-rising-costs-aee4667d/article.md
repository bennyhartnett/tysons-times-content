Fairfax County Supervisor Walter Alcorn tackled concerns ranging from courthouse immigration enforcement to a disputed $150 million school property purchase during a June 30 summer town hall in Vienna.

The Hunter Mill District Democrat fielded questions on countywide issues including taxes, federal job losses, public safety staffing, trash collection, affordable housing and data centers. He also offered an update on the Reston Town Center North redevelopment.

## School property purchase raises concerns

One of the sharpest discussions centered on Fairfax County Public Schools’ planned $150 million purchase of the King Abdullah Academy property near Herndon. The deal was scheduled to close Aug. 15.

The site can accommodate roughly 450 to 475 students, far fewer than the 2,000 to 2,500 typically enrolled at a county high school. Residents questioned the acquisition because the recently renovated Herndon High School remains under capacity.

Alcorn said he did not learn about the pending purchase until May and described the situation as “very concerning.” He said renovation costs remained unknown and FCPS had not publicly explained how it intended to use the property.

“I don't know what their plan is, to be honest,” he said.

Although the Board of Supervisors does not control school spending, it approves the school bonds used to finance improvements.

The purchase comes as FCPS addressed an estimated $300 million challenge in its fiscal 2026 budget. Alcorn pointed to potential savings from combining some county and school technology services, with committee meetings expected in the fall. He also said a facilities committee is working to improve coordination between the School Board and supervisors.

Virginia requires balanced budgets, Alcorn noted, leaving officials to cut spending, raise taxes or use a combination of both.

## ICE activity and emergency staffing

Discussing an ICE operation at the Fairfax County courthouse, Alcorn said county police and the sheriff’s office do not assist federal immigration authorities but also do not obstruct them.

He said the county’s trust policy is intended to make residents comfortable contacting police regardless of immigration status, which officials view as important to public safety.

Alcorn also reported that competitive compensation and collective bargaining have helped Fairfax fully staff its police and fire departments. A serious paramedic shortage remains, however, and he said the county will seek public input as it considers solutions.

## Taxes, federal cuts and housing pressures

Alcorn agreed with residents who said Fairfax County real estate taxes are too high and can make it difficult for young adults to establish themselves in the county. He said he is looking for ways to reduce the cost of providing local services.

Federal spending and workforce reductions are adding pressure. Alcorn said Congress was considering cutting nearly $1 billion from federal affordable-housing funding administered through the Department of Housing and Urban Development. County employees were evaluating how federal changes could shift new expenses and responsibilities to Fairfax.

He also described efforts by the Fairfax County Economic Development Authority to help people affected by federal job cuts. Northern Virginia Community College has offered free courses to laid-off federal employees and contractors.

Available jobs in the region fell from 95,000 in January to 40,000 in March before recovering to 48,000, according to figures Alcorn presented.

## Trash collection and deer control

Fairfax could eventually consider creating a unified sanitation district that assigns haulers by area instead of allowing multiple companies to serve the same neighborhoods. Such a system is legal in Virginia, but no county plan has been developed.

State law requires a five-year waiting period before implementation. Alcorn said that delay could provide valuable planning time.

He was less enthusiastic about “pay as you throw” systems that charge residents according to the amount of trash they discard. Similar programs elsewhere have struggled with enforcement and declining recycling when residents switch to smaller bins, he said.

On wildlife management, Alcorn said Fairfax’s deer-culling program uses off-duty police officers on Park Authority property. It does not extend to private land controlled by organizations such as the Reston Association.

Culling has reduced deer populations in some locations, though the practice remains debated. Alcorn acknowledged that the county has few alternatives and identified sterilization as another possible way to control deer numbers and associated concerns such as Lyme disease.

## Reston redevelopment moves forward

Plans for Reston Town Center North involve a complicated exchange of land between Fairfax County and Inova. The proposed swap would clear the way for a new regional library, homeless shelter and human services center.

Officials will make building design decisions after rezoning. The public facilities are expected to be developed alongside at least 1,000 new homes.

## Data center rules draw scrutiny

Alcorn called for data centers to require special exceptions rather than being permitted automatically under by-right zoning.

“I don't like them by right in any zoning district,” he said.

Fairfax is tightening its data center regulations, revising zoning standards to limit neighborhood and environmental effects, and reviewing rules governing electrical substations. Industry representatives have warned that special-exception reviews could delay projects.

Power-capacity constraints have already reduced the scale of proposed data centers along the Silver Line, shifting more attention toward the Route 28 corridor in the Sully District.

The facilities provide substantial tax revenue while creating relatively few jobs. Loudoun County has accepted that trade-off as a way to support lower tax rates, but Alcorn said Fairfax must weigh the revenue against zoning and environmental burdens.
