Gulf Branch Fall Festival turned everyday life in early Arlington into a hands-on experience, inviting visitors to make butter, press cider, sew fabric and experiment with natural dyes.

At the butter-making stand, Rachael Tolman shaped a freshly churned mound while explaining that the process requires only heavy whipping cream, with salt added afterward if desired. Visitors lined up for baguette slices topped with the finished butter as a young volunteer worked a pottery churn by moving its lever up and down. Producing a batch takes about an hour.

Nearby, children pushed the handles of an apple grinder, crushing fruit from local orchards for cider. Volunteers cut the apples into chunks before feeding them into the crank-operated press. Nothing else was added; visitors could sample the cider plain or flavored with cinnamon, nutmeg, cloves and allspice.

Traditional textile work filled several other tables. Guests selected brightly colored fabric squares and learned to stitch around their edges, with a completed quilt displayed as an example. At another station, volunteers helped visitors weave different yarns into miniature carpets. Lauren Shrader of Maryland, who attended with two friends, volunteered at the yarn table.

A natural-dyeing activity made its festival debut on the terrace above the stone steps. Erin Cruz showed visitors black walnuts gathered from a nearby tree, which can turn fabric brown, and pokeberries that create pinkish-purple coloring. Other dyes came from native plants and ingredients such as turmeric, which produces yellow. Samples also demonstrated how different fabrics absorb dye with varying results.

Children tried another early skill by writing and drawing with feather quills and ink made from plant, animal or mineral extracts. Several quickly discovered that controlling a quill was harder than it looked.

Across the terrace, pumpkin decorating encouraged a more imaginative approach. Evelyn Platt, who walked to the festival from her nearby neighborhood, presented her bright red creation to her mother and said she was making it look “really bloody.”

Beyond the organized activities, the creek offered its own timeless attraction. Children scrambled across the rocks and tossed twigs into the flowing water.
