Steam, history and the aromas of a holiday feast fill the Hendersons’ Alexandria home each Thanksgiving. Collard greens simmer beside a honey-and-herb roasted turkey as relatives and longtime friends prepare dishes throughout the kitchen.

“This is what home smells like,” Alicia Henderson said as her sister, Danielle Bolger, arranged olive oil-glazed root vegetables after driving in from Fairfax.

The gathering is known as Friendsgiving at the Yard, a name combining the familiar holiday tradition with the Yard—the central gathering place where students socialize on historically Black college campuses.

It began more than 25 years ago among recent Spelman College and Morehouse College graduates who belonged to historically Black fraternities and sororities of the Divine Nine. Unable to afford flights home for Thanksgiving, the friends decided to celebrate together.

Their first meal reflected their limited budgets: boxed macaroni and cheese, frozen turkey bought for 49 cents a pound and a pumpkin pie from Giant.

Much has changed since then. Freezer-aisle turkeys have given way to heritage, free-range birds, while studio apartments have been replaced by homes as large as 8,000 square feet. The gathering’s foundation, however, remains rooted in friendship, family and heritage.

In the kitchen, the friends work with the familiarity built over decades, exchanging pans and seasoning dishes by instinct. Sweet potatoes caramelize beneath a torch, while Bolger serves homemade cranberry sauce flavored with orange peel and cinnamon bark. Laughter flows among people who already know where every mixing bowl is stored.

As the couples had children, the gathering gained another ritual. Before eating, everyone pauses for what the families call “the context.” The practice began as a joke after Marcus Henderson was teased for turning meals into history lessons.

“Most people talk about the Mayflower this time of year,” Marcus said. “But our story starts before that. It began with the Africans who arrived in Virginia in 1619 on the White Lion and built communities long before the Pilgrims showed up.”

For the families, the lesson is not meant to erase Thanksgiving history. It is a way to reclaim the holiday by including the history that shaped their own families and communities.

“We wanted our kids to know that we’ve always been here,” Alicia said. “That their roots go deep, deeper than any textbook will tell them.”

That celebration of heritage continues after the meal. One year, dinner gave way to an impromptu step show, drawing on the synchronized movements and chants historically Black fraternities and sororities use to express organizational pride.

“We can’t perform like we did in college. We’ve got bad knees and might throw out our backs, but we still know how to make the Yard shake,” Marcus said with a laugh.

Friendsgiving at the Yard is neither formal nor carefully orchestrated. It unfolds naturally each year, sustained by the friendships, memories and history that have kept the tradition alive for more than a quarter-century.
