Gunston Hall entered a pivotal anniversary year with new leadership as Dr. Christopher Eck became executive director of the historic Mason Neck property in January.

Eck said sites such as Gunston Hall give visitors a tangible connection to the people, places and ideas that shaped the United States. That connection carries added significance in 2026, the 250th anniversary of both the nation’s founding and George Mason’s Virginia Declaration of Rights.

Eck brings more than two decades of experience in historic preservation, archaeology and public administration across academia and federal and local government.

Before joining Gunston Hall, he led the National Historical Publications and Records Commission at the National Archives. There, he oversaw grants supporting the preservation and digitization of historical records outside the federal government, including George Washington’s papers.

A registered professional archaeologist, Eck has also held preservation and cultural-resources positions with the U.S. departments of Defense and Commerce, the General Services Administration and metropolitan governments.

Raised in Atlanta, Eck studied history at Loyola University in New Orleans. He later earned a master’s degree in historical archaeology from the University of Massachusetts and a law degree from the University of Miami. He has also taught historic-preservation law at the university level.

Eck lives near Warrenton with his wife and their two college-age children.

## A Full Calendar for the 250th Anniversary

Gunston Hall will mark 250 years since the adoption of the Virginia Declaration of Rights on June 12, 1776, with special programs and public events throughout 2026.

Plans include a March screening of a film about 18th-century African American writer Phillis Wheatley. The production was filmed at Gunston Hall and George Washington’s Mount Vernon estate.

Other scheduled events include a gala on May 16 and speakers on July 4. The “Families in Focus” series will explore both the Mason family and the enslaved families who lived at Gunston Hall.

The site will continue offering colonial food demonstrations, history talks, candlelit cocktail events, 18th-century game nights, school and public tours, and nature walks.

Eck also works with Gunston Hall’s Descendants Advisory Board, whose members trace their ancestry to people enslaved at the plantation.

## George Mason’s Complicated Legacy

Gunston Hall was the Mason family plantation, beginning with George Mason IV’s great-grandfather, George Mason I, who left England after the Royalists were defeated in the 1650s.

George Mason IV helped develop the Fairfax Resolves, which asserted the rights of the British colonists. He also helped write the Virginia Declaration of Rights, a 16-article document that advanced individual liberties including freedom of religion and freedom of the press.

The declaration influenced the Declaration of Independence and the Bill of Rights later added to the U.S. Constitution.

Mason refused to sign the Constitution, largely because it did not contain a bill of rights. Those amendments were adopted in 1791, one year before his death.

His legacy also contains a fundamental contradiction. Mason promoted individual rights and supported independence from the British monarchy, yet he enslaved people, lived in a home constructed by enslaved laborers and never freed those he held in bondage.

Mason spent his life on Mason Neck, with his journey to Philadelphia for the Constitutional Convention representing the farthest he traveled, according to Eck. Though he rarely ventured far from home, Mason read extensively and engaged with thinkers including Montesquieu, the French philosopher associated with the theory of separated governmental powers.

Today, Gunston Hall is an accredited museum, a National Historic Landmark and a popular visitor destination. Its remaining 554 acres represent about one-tenth of the original plantation.

The property includes the Mason family’s 3,200-square-foot mansion, several outbuildings, a restored garden dating to the 1780s and the foundation remains of a barn.

Virginia Nicholson, Gunston Hall’s first regent, said Eck’s background in preservation, archaeology and strategic leadership made him well suited to guide the site’s next period of growth.

For Eck, the opportunity to help preserve the legacy of one of the country’s founders is “phenomenal.”
