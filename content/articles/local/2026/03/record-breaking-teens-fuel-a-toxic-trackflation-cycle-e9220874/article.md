High school track and field records keep falling, but each extraordinary performance raises expectations for young runners whose own elite times can suddenly seem inadequate.

The growing phenomenon, sometimes called “trackflation,” encourages athletes to measure themselves against rare, record-setting talents. Those comparisons can cause nationally competitive runners to discount how accomplished they already are.

Sixteen-year-old Sam Ruthe of New Zealand demonstrated just how exceptional the new benchmarks have become. On Jan. 31, he completed the mile in 3:48.88 at the John Thomas Terrier Classic in Boston, setting an under-18 world record.

Ruthe’s performance was only about five seconds slower than the overall mile world record of 3:43.13, set by Morocco’s Hicham El Guerrouj at age 24 in Rome on July 7, 1999.

For comparison, the average mile time for a 16-year-old boy is about 5:30, while an average 5K takes approximately 20 minutes. Ruthe is among only a handful of boys his age to have broken four minutes in the mile.

His achievement deserves recognition, but using it as an ordinary standard can distort how other runners view their abilities. High school athletes with times far above average may still conclude that they are not good enough when comparing themselves with Ruthe.

The same tension surrounds 400-meter standout Quincey Wilson. He set the under-18 world record of 44.10 seconds at age 17 during the 2025 Ed Murphey Classic, improving on his 44.20 mark from 2024. Wilson also won an Olympic relay gold medal with Team USA.

Wilson received recognition before and after his appearance in the 400 meters at the New Balance Indoor Nationals. Yet the greater the attention on performances like his, the easier it becomes for other talented runners to treat an exceptional outlier as the new expectation.

That pressure was visible against the backdrop of the national meet, where McLean’s girls 4×800-meter relay team competed after finishing second at the Indoor Regionals.

Senior Katy Zang of Ohio delivered another standout performance at New Balance Indoor Nationals on March 12, breaking the national high school women’s two-mile record with a time of 9:37.15. Her pace averaged less than five minutes per mile.

An average two-mile time for a high school girl is about 12 minutes, underscoring how unusual Zang’s result was. Performances by athletes such as Zang, Wilson and Ruthe should be celebrated without becoming the standard by which every promising runner judges personal success.

For emerging athletes, the more useful competition is against their own previous performances. Even as records continue to fall, improvement and nationally competitive results remain worthy achievements.
