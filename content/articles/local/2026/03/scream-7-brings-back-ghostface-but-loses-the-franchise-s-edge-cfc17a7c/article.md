After decades of reshaping the horror genre, the *Scream* franchise returns with another blood-soaked slasher—but *Scream 7* shows troubling signs that the series is running out of sharp ideas.

Ghostface remains an old-school killer, wielding the bloody knife that has become the franchise’s signature symbol rather than relying on a gun. The new installment delivers plenty of gore and introduces additional characters, yet its story never matches the intensity of its attacks.

Neve Campbell returns as Sidney Prescott, while Courteney Cox reprises her role as Gale Weathers. Isabel May joins the cast as Sidney’s daughter, Tatum Evans, whose name honors Sidney’s best friend from the original film.

The production faced major disruption before reaching theaters. Melissa Barrera was removed from the project after making pro-Palestinian social media posts, and Jenna Ortega later departed as well. With their characters having played central roles in the previous two movies, their absence leaves a noticeable hole in the narrative.

The film also struggles to make effective use of the actors who did return. Gale appears early but is absent during the climax, resurfacing only near the end. Other familiar characters briefly arrive, suffer injuries and disappear before abruptly returning for the finale. These entrances and exits make the story feel fragmented.

Even the central mystery—traditionally a defining strength of the series—disappoints. Ghostface’s eventual unmasking comes with a motive that feels rushed and thin compared with the more carefully developed revelations of earlier installments.

Matthew Lillard’s Stu Macher also returns in an unusual AI-related form. Instead of providing a clever connection to the original movie, the concept feels forced and creates more confusion than excitement.

The horror sequences are far more effective. Several inventive and brutal attacks preserve the franchise’s trademark tension, giving fans of its graphic violence some memorable moments even when the larger story falters.

Visually, the camera work and cinematography are competent but unremarkable. The movie lacks the distinctive energy and style that helped earlier entries leave a lasting impression.

Ultimately, *Scream 7* relies heavily on familiar faces and nostalgic callbacks without building a strong enough story around them. Its gore may satisfy longtime viewers, but weak motivations, limited character development and uneven continuity place it among the franchise’s least successful installments.

Earlier movies demonstrated that *Scream* could evolve through a new generation of characters and stories. This sequel instead feels like a step backward—and proof that even Ghostface’s blade can lose its edge.
