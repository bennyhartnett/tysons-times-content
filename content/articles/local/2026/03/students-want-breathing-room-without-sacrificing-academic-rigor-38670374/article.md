Student burnout rarely stems from one difficult class. It builds during weeks when tests, projects, homework, extracurricular activities and college-related pressures converge.

Juniors face concerns about grades and testing, while seniors balance college decisions, leadership responsibilities and graduation requirements. Academic achievement remains important, but relentless exhaustion can push curiosity out of the classroom.

“A lot of the stress comes from everything piling up at the same time; tests, activities and college pressure,” junior April Ibarra said. “[The stress] is not just one thing, it’s the way everything overlaps.”

Around one in four students in grades eight through 12 report high stress and mental health challenges during the school year, according to the Fairfax County Youth Survey. Sustained pressure can disrupt sleep, weaken concentration and reduce students’ engagement at school.

The discussion should go beyond identifying burnout. Schools also need to consider how they respond when students become overwhelmed. From the student perspective, the problem is not always the amount of work—it is often the limited flexibility available when major obligations collide.

“From what I have observed over the years, burnout is always high around [third quarter],” English teacher Michael Enos said. “I need to keep the course challenging and rigorous, but I also know that students have a lot going on in their lives.”

Perfectly coordinating assignments across every department may be unrealistic. A more practical response would be to build structured flexibility into school policies. Students could be allowed to discuss alternative test dates, request extensions when several major assignments overlap or take occasional mental health days without academic penalties.

Such accommodations would not necessarily reduce the workload. Instead, they would give students more control over when they complete it. Even motivated students can struggle to perform well when several major assessments land in the same week.

Flexibility would also need clear limits. Teachers must treat students fairly and prevent deadlines from becoming optional. Requiring advance notice or restricting the number of extensions could preserve accountability while offering relief when pressure peaks.

Advisory periods and study blocks may help students organize their responsibilities, but those supports cannot solve the problem alone. Students also need communication, understanding and reasonable leeway when overlapping deadlines become unmanageable.

“Sometimes the hardest part isn’t the work itself, but when everything is due at the same time,” senior Faith Chasse said. “If teachers coordinated deadlines more, it would make a huge difference.”

Schools do not have to choose between strong academic standards and student well-being. Keeping coursework rigorous while allowing carefully structured deadline or testing adjustments could make success more attainable—and improve how students experience school each day.
