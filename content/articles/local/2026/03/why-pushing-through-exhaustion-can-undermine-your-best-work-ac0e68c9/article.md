Rest is not a luxury. It is a requirement for performing well at work, supporting a cause, volunteering and showing up responsibly for friends and family.

Heather Cox Richardson captured that idea during her Feb. 19 Politics Chat when she encouraged anyone needing a break to take one, assuring listeners that others would still be there.

Yet stepping away can feel unproductive, uncomfortable or even irresponsible. Understanding why rest is difficult can make it easier to recognize what is getting in the way.

## Why slowing down feels so difficult

Some people struggle to switch off because their thoughts continue racing after they stop working. They may remain focused on current events, important causes or unfinished responsibilities even when physically tired.

Constant sensory input can compound the problem. Noise, artificial light, notifications and algorithm-driven content create few opportunities to disconnect completely.

Stillness itself may also be uncomfortable. A life filled with work, errands, communication and social media can make quiet moments feel wasteful. Slowing down may bring stress, loneliness, boredom, worry or self-doubt to the surface, turning busyness into a coping mechanism.

Identity and guilt can create another barrier. People who connect their self-worth to productivity or helping others may interpret downtime as failure. Years of pressure to accomplish more can make rest seem lazy or undeserved.

Neurodivergent people may experience these challenges more intensely. Masking, executive-function demands, social cues and difficulty processing stimuli can make rest feel like yet another task.

The obstacle, then, may not simply be a crowded schedule. It may be the deeper pressures and emotions driving that schedule.

## Small ways to begin resting

One starting point is to spend 60 seconds sitting quietly—or listening to a calming soundscape—and breathing normally. The goal is not to master a technique but to notice what thoughts and feelings emerge.

A person worried about an overwhelming inbox, for example, might examine that worry more closely. Instead of “a million emails,” there may be 20 important messages, 10 that can wait and many that require no response. Looking at the situation more accurately may make it feel manageable.

Simple exercises are not always enough, however. Difficulty resting can be connected to identity, guilt, past trauma or discomfort with stillness. Licensed professional support may help when self-soothing and reframing techniques are ineffective, take increasingly long to work or are needed more frequently.

Falls Church and surrounding communities offer mental-health resources. Workplace Employee Assistance Programs may also provide an entry point for finding support.

It can also help to redefine rest. The wellness industry sometimes presents recovery as another costly obligation, but rest does not need to involve products, appointments or elaborate routines.

Even while someone sits on a couch, the body continues performing essential functions. Basal metabolic rate—the energy needed to sustain the body at rest—typically accounts for roughly 60% to 70% of daily calorie use. Inactivity is not the same as the body doing nothing.

## A break does not abandon the cause

Rest can be especially difficult for people devoted to a profession, movement or community effort. Stepping away may feel as though it weakens the mission, particularly when every participant’s contribution matters.

But burnout also limits a person’s ability to contribute. Teams and communities need their members to care for themselves so they can return and remain involved over time.

The scientific idea of resonant interaction offers a useful comparison: When oscillating systems align, they reinforce one another instead of canceling each other out. Collective work can similarly produce more than individuals could accomplish alone—but only when participants can continue contributing.

Taking time to recover today may be what makes it possible to return to important commitments tomorrow.
