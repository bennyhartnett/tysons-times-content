Three dazzling ballroom gowns from an Annandale showroom are headed to the Smithsonian this spring, bringing the intricate artistry of dance costumes into a national exhibition on salsa.

The dresses come from Julie Wilson’s Encore Ballroom Couture, where more than 1,000 gowns fill racks with feathers, crystals and vivid colors. Dancers from around the world visit the business to buy, consign or rent its performance attire.

Beginning April 18, the selected gowns will appear in *¡Puro Ritmo! The Musical Journey of Salsa* at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of the American Latino in Washington, D.C. The free exhibition will trace the history, cultural influence and evolution of salsa music and dance in the United States through fashion, artifacts and performance history.

The three gowns were created by the late Randall Christensen, an Emmy Award-winning costume designer who helped define the modern appearance of ballroom performance. Wilson said their selection recognizes a specialized craft combining fine artistry with the engineering required for athletic movement.

Ballroom gowns are constructed to respond to every turn, dip and lift. Crystals are applied individually, beads are arranged with the care of jewelry making, and fabric panels are cut to accommodate a dancer’s speed and range of motion.

Wilson said the dresses embody salsa’s color, energy and heat, telling a story before a dancer even begins to move.

The donation also carries personal significance for Wilson, who spent much of her childhood visiting the Smithsonian with her mother, Brenda S. Wilson. She is honoring her mother’s memory through the gift while helping preserve Christensen’s artistic legacy.

“It has always been a dream to donate an artifact to the Smithsonian,” Wilson said. “Seeing these gowns on exhibit will be a dream realized — and a way to honor the people who shaped my life.”

Wilson hopes the exhibition encourages visitors to view ballroom clothing as part of the country’s wider cultural history. Popular forms of social dance have shifted with American tastes, identities and communities, from early 20th-century styles and disco to the renewed popularity of line dancing.

“Dance will always be woven through the fabric of our lives,” she said. “It is relatable, it is a fantasy, it is you, it is me.”

The gowns are believed to be the first ballroom dance dresses displayed at the Smithsonian, giving Wilson’s years of preserving performance couture a place in history.

## Visiting the Exhibition

*¡Puro Ritmo! The Musical Journey of Salsa* opens April 18 at the Smithsonian’s National Museum of the American Latino in Washington. Admission is free, and the museum is open daily from 10 a.m. to 5:30 p.m.
