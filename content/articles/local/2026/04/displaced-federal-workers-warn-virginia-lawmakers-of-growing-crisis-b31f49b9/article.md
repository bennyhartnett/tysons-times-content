Former federal employees told Northern Virginia lawmakers on April 7 that abrupt job losses have left many struggling to find work, secure promised benefits and navigate a scattered network of assistance programs.

The Alexandria meeting brought together the Alexandria Area Federal Employee Alumni Support Network and state legislators and staff. Participants said the Trump administration’s actions had unexpectedly ended federal careers across agencies including FEMA, HHS, the Department of Agriculture, GSA, USAID and the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

The affected workers included contractors, postdoctoral employees, probationary staff and permanent employees with decades of service. Their positions disappeared through grant cuts, reductions in force and deferred resignation programs. Some have since found jobs, but many remain unemployed, received no severance or are still waiting for retirement benefits.

“We feel lost in the chaos and damage that’s being done,” one participant said.

Sen. Elizabeth Bennett-Parker and Delegates Kirk McPike, Garrett McGuire and Paul Krizek attended in person. House Leader Charniele Herring and Senate Leader Scott Surovell were represented by their chiefs of staff.

## Specialized experience becomes a barrier

Participants said years spent developing highly specialized government expertise have made the shift to private-sector employment unexpectedly difficult. Many need help explaining how their federal experience applies elsewhere or acquiring skills for entirely different careers.

They also described age discrimination and job openings attracting thousands of applicants. Some are pursuing positions for which they are overqualified that pay substantially less than their former federal salaries.

“Please remember the talent and the human cost of not having a place to land,” the group urged lawmakers.

Workers said assistance is available through state and local governments and nonprofit organizations, but finding and navigating those services can be difficult. They asked legislators to coordinate with localities and the Spanberger administration, simplify access and better publicize existing resources.

The federal Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act program received praise as a “shining star” because it offers resources and grants that can help workers retrain. Participants warned, however, that the program is “crushed under the number of federal workers” seeking assistance and needs more General Assembly funding.

Reviews were considerably worse for the Metropolitan Washington Council of Governments’ Talent Capital Initiative, an AI-based job-matching service. The House budget includes another $200,000 for the project, but users at the meeting described it as “messy” and “glitchy.”

One participant said the platform directed them to already-filled jobs in Turkey. Workers argued that any additional funding should depend on improvements informed by user feedback—if the service receives more money at all.

## Lawmakers point to new protections

The group also discussed a Virginia House emergency committee that examined federal cuts throughout the previous year. Its work produced a report with practical recommendations for helping displaced federal employees.

None of the lawmakers at the Alexandria meeting had served on that committee, and they could not say whether its specific recommendations had been implemented. They did identify related measures passed during the legislative session, including:

- Eviction protections for renters under HB837 and SB273, HB527 and SB628, and HB15 and SB48;
- Higher unemployment benefits through SB759 and HB1320;
- Preferential state-government hiring for former federal employees under HB494;
- A provision invalidating noncompete clauses when workers receive no severance under SB170.

Krizek said efforts to help displaced federal employees had been difficult under the previous Youngkin administration. He attributed the fragmented response partly to local governments having to establish services independently without support from the governor.

He expressed greater optimism about the Spanberger administration, which quickly formed a new task force to address the problem.

Lawmakers acknowledged that legislation moves too slowly to resolve many of the workers’ immediate needs. They offered to assist constituents more directly by writing job-reference letters, recommending small-business startups for state contracts and helping people reach state services.

They also encouraged constituents to copy their offices on communications with local workforce-development staff. Seeing those exchanges, lawmakers said, could help them determine which services are working and which are failing.

## Fears of a mental health emergency

The meeting repeatedly exposed the emotional toll of the job losses. Several participants struggled through tears while describing the importance of their former work, their treatment by DOGE and the Trump administration, and the sense of invalidation and disorientation that followed.

Workers warned that the upheaval is creating a broader mental health emergency. One participant said that without prompt assistance, the situation could develop into a “federal worker suicide crisis.”
