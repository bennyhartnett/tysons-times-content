A days-old kitten trapped inside a car engine became one of the first signs of another demanding kitten season for Northern Virginia animal rescuers.

Amy Schindler, chief operating officer of the Animal Welfare League of Arlington, received the call on a warm March afternoon. An animal control officer needed help with the fragile kitten, who had serious respiratory problems and required care around the clock.

Schindler took him home to foster, naming him Otto with her roommate. Cats and kittens frequently crawl into engines, wheel wells and other confined spaces for warmth, she said. Otto was rescued after someone heard him meowing.

His arrival reflected a challenge that local shelters say no longer fits neatly within a single season.

Unspayed female cats give birth more frequently as temperatures rise and daylight increases, causing the kitten population to surge. In Northern Virginia, rescuers now see new litters well into October.

Schindler said breeding generally slows only when colder weather pushes outdoor cats to focus on finding shelter. Tails High Rescue, a nonprofit foster-based organization, reports a similar pattern.

Acacia Waller, president of the Tails High board, said December through February brings only a slight reprieve. The work of accepting kittens and arranging spay-and-neuter services continues throughout the year.

The stakes are particularly high for kittens born outside. Waller said only 25% survive without human intervention.

## Fragile kittens require specialized care

The Animal Welfare League of Arlington and Tails High each care for hundreds of cats annually, including animals from across Northern Virginia and West Virginia.

Both organizations have experience with neonatal kittens, generally defined as newborns through about four weeks old. These “bottle babies” need carefully controlled warmth, specialized nutrition and frequent feeding. Orphaned newborns may require trained caregivers capable of bottle, syringe or tube feeding.

The Arlington shelter’s kitten program relies on experienced staff and a large foster network. Because Arlington has fewer stray cats than many rural communities, the organization also transfers in orphaned and neonatal kittens from shelters and rescuers elsewhere in Virginia, West Virginia and the District.

Tails High similarly assigns the youngest kittens to its most experienced volunteers. Newer fosters may begin with weaned kittens, which still need nurturing but not the same intensive, round-the-clock attention. The rescue uses equipment such as incubators to maintain the temperatures and environments that newborn kittens need.

The organization also assists orphaned kittens, mothers with litters, friendly strays and cats requiring urgent medical treatment.

## Before moving kittens, look for their mother

Rescuers say people who discover kittens outdoors should first determine whether their mother is nearby.

Mother cats routinely leave litters for several hours while searching for food. They may hide kittens beneath decks, on porches, in window wells or in other warm, sheltered places.

Unless the kittens face immediate danger—such as being beside a busy road, inside a dumpster or trapped in a vehicle—Waller recommends watching the area and consulting a rescue organization before intervening.

Removing healthy kittens too quickly can separate them from a mother who was still caring for them. It can also leave the unspayed mother outdoors to produce more litters.

Schindler said residents are often advised to wait and watch because the mother may return. Truly orphaned kittens, however, need prompt supportive care. Rescue organizations can help callers assess which situation they have encountered.

Kittens should not be given cow’s milk or goat milk. Both organizations stressed that newborns need their mother’s milk or a formula made specifically for kittens. Improper feeding can make them seriously ill.

## Identifying outdoor cats can speed the response

Rescuers also encourage callers to distinguish among stray, feral and community cats.

Strays are typically former pets that became lost or were abandoned. Feral cats are unsocialized and generally unsuitable for life as household pets. Community cats live primarily outdoors and receive care without having a documented owner.

An outdoor cat with a clipped or “ear-tipped” ear has usually been spayed or neutered through a Trap-Neuter-Return program. That visible marker can help rescuers understand whether a colony is already being managed.

Waller said callers can provide valuable information by speaking with neighbors or checking neighborhood forums and local email lists. A cat that initially appears abandoned may already receive food and health monitoring from several households.

Unsocialized colonies without ear-tipped cats are more likely to produce kittens. Waller called Trap-Neuter-Return a critical tool and urged residents who feed outdoor cats to help arrange sterilization. Local animal welfare organizations offer training, traps and low- or no-cost services.

Community cats are common in Arlington, where an outdoor cat may be returned to its environment only when at least two feeding sources have been confirmed, according to Schindler.

The county’s large and frequently changing rental population can complicate those cases. Indoor-outdoor cats may be left behind when residents move, then continue receiving food from new neighbors.

When a mother cat enters the shelter with no known history, staff must assess whether she can be adopted or should be spayed and returned to an established outdoor community.

## One cat can produce nearly 20 kittens

The numbers can grow quickly. A fertile cat may have two or three litters in a season, with four to six kittens in each. One mother can therefore produce nearly 20 kittens within several months.

Otto, the kitten rescued from the car engine, ultimately did not survive. Schindler said he nevertheless spent most of his short life receiving physical care and affection.

The Arlington shelter reports a 97% live-release rate, with hundreds of animals saved each year. Tails High also says most kittens entering its program survive and are adopted. Cats that remain longer are often receiving hospice care or need additional time because they are extremely shy.

Tails High keeps kittens until they are at least eight weeks old, allowing time for deworming, vaccinations, other veterinary care and spaying or neutering before adoption.

Both organizations depend heavily on community support. The Animal Welfare League of Arlington trains fosters for cats of different ages and can work with varying schedules. Tails High generally seeks foster commitments of at least one month.

Residents who cannot foster can still donate, volunteer or share adoption posts. As Waller put it, even a small action that helps one cat find a home can have a lasting effect.
