Federal officials are asking the public to report suspicious activity at Fort Hunt Park after years of vandalism left graffiti across its historic concrete artillery batteries.

Park employees have found painted letters, drawings and smears on the porous 19th-century structures. In April, National Park Service rangers, preservation experts and maintenance crews from around the country removed the markings while working to protect the original material.

Crews used low-pressure washing, hand scrubbing, non-acidic cleaning products and repeated rinsing. The National Park Service said the cleanup restored a welcoming setting for the thousands of people who use the park each year.

Anyone who sees suspicious or criminal activity can contact U.S. Park Police at 202-379-4877 or USPP_TIPline@nps.gov. Reports should include as much detail as possible.

Fort Hunt Park sits in the Mount Vernon area, about five miles south of Alexandria along the southern George Washington Memorial Parkway. Part of the National Park Service-managed 25-mile parkway, it now draws visitors for recreation, picnics and community events.

## A Site Spanning Thousands of Years

Indigenous people hunted and fished in the Fort Hunt area from about 6,000 B.C. through the mid-1700s. English explorer Captain John Smith encountered members of the Conoy tribe there in 1608. The land later became part of George Washington’s Mount Vernon plantation, where he established a community for enslaved people described as a “slave village.”

Concerns raised by federal officials in 1885 about the country’s vulnerability to naval attack eventually led the War Department to build several artillery batteries at Fort Hunt between 1898 and 1904. Intended to defend the Potomac River and Washington during the Spanish-American War era, the fort was named for Brevet Major General Henry Jackson Hunt, who lived from 1819 to 1889.

The batteries’ semicircular tops allowed their guns to rotate. Shells weighing several hundred pounds could be lifted from below using elevators.

Fort Hunt’s battery commander’s tower, completed in 1902 with concrete walls more than two feet thick, remains largely as it originally appeared. Its elevated position allowed a commander to watch the Potomac and coordinate fire among the batteries.

The property later served as a Civilian Conservation Corps camp during the Great Depression. Two aligned rows of trees planted by the corps in the 1930s still trace the former service road. A surviving pin oak commemorates the 1939 visit of King George VI and Queen Elizabeth.

From 1942 through 1946, Fort Hunt housed a secret World War II interrogation operation concealed behind the deliberately ordinary designation P.O. Box 1142. About 4,500 German, Japanese and Italian prisoners of war passed through the camp, including Nazi naval officers and German scientists.

Most buildings associated with that operation were later demolished. Grassy fields, wooded spaces and busy picnic pavilions now occupy much of the area, though four concrete guard-tower bases remain. The park service said removing the graffiti helps preserve a landscape that reflects both Fort Hunt’s history and its natural setting.
