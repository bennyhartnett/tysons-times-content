Fairfax County residents can now explore the local court system and its civil rights history through a new online education hub believed to be the first of its kind offered by a state trial court in the United States.

The Fairfax County Circuit Court officially launched its Judicial Learning Center on June 17. Designed for students and the broader public, the website explains how courts operate while examining Fairfax cases that helped shape civil rights law.

Clerk of Court Chris Falcon said the center complements the county’s Court Tour Program and highlights local cases that contributed to a more just society. It also gives residents a new way to engage with the judiciary and Fairfax County history from home.

The circuit court developed the center with the Fairfax Bar Association, Fairfax County Public Schools, George Mason University and the Center for Mason Legacies, a university research center focused on George Mason and local history.

A committee of judges, lawyers, public servants, researchers and other professionals began developing the project in 2024. Over two years, the group worked with an exhibit design firm to create material inspired by displays at the Supreme Court of Virginia and the Fairfax Bar Association’s courthouse tours.

Established in 1976, the tour program introduces middle school students to the justice system through a mock trial, conversations with attorneys and judges, and observation of an actual trial. The online center can reinforce classroom civics lessons before or after students participate.

The website debuted with two exhibits. One covers the structure of the American judicial system, the people who work within it and the documents on which it is founded. The other examines two civil rights disputes that began in Fairfax Circuit Court: the Carpenter and Little Hunting Park cases.

The 1935 Carpenter case addressed defendants’ right to trial by a jury of their peers. The Little Hunting Park case arose after Dr. Theodore Freeman, a Black resident renting a home in 1965, was denied membership in the neighborhood swimming pool club.

That dispute eventually reached the U.S. Supreme Court, which ruled that racial discrimination connected to the ownership and use of property is unlawful. The court also found that a person cannot be punished for challenging such discrimination.

Visitors can additionally access archival work completed by interns at the circuit court’s Historic Records Center.

The court plans to expand the project with physical exhibits at the Fairfax County Courthouse for students and members of the public.

The learning center joins several court-access initiatives introduced since Falcon’s 2023 election. Those efforts include a free online system launched in 2024 offering basic civil and criminal case information, as well as a satellite location providing certain court services.
