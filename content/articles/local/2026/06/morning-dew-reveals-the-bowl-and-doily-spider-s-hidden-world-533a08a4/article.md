Morning dew can expose an intricate world that usually hides in plain sight: the delicate, two-tiered web of the Bowl and Doily Spider (*Frontinella pyramitela*).

Common across the United States, these spiders build their webs in weedy fields, shrubs and bushes. The fine silk can be nearly invisible until droplets of dew or rain collect along its strands.

The web’s unusual design gives the species its name. An inverted, rounded “bowl” hangs above a horizontal sheet of silk resembling a doily. The spider clings beneath the bowl, protected by the layered structure while waiting for gnats, mosquitoes and small flies.

When an insect enters the web, the spider races toward it and injects venom that paralyzes the prey. Although lethal to small insects, the venom is not dangerous to humans.

Bowl and Doily Spiders are tiny, measuring only about three to four millimeters. Their bodies are reddish brown, and their legs have a yellowish color.

A close look in spring will typically reveal a lone female occupying the web. By late summer, a male may join her during an extended mating period. This shared living arrangement is unusual among generally solitary spiders. Males do not construct their own webs, instead relying on females for shelter and access to food.

The best time to find these hidden homes is early in the morning, while dew still outlines the silk. As sunlight dries the droplets, the webs appear to vanish—even though they remain suspended among the vegetation.
