Olive Young opened its first official U.S. store in Los Angeles on May 29, giving South Korea’s leading beauty retailer a physical foothold in a fast-growing American market.

The opportunity is substantial. K-beauty products generated an estimated $2.4 billion in U.S. sales last year, suggesting Olive Young could eventually challenge major retailers such as Sephora and Ulta Beauty. But rising demand alone will not guarantee success.

To build a lasting American presence, Olive Young must understand the country’s varied beauty preferences while preserving the products and qualities that made it successful in South Korea.

Those preferences can differ sharply between the two markets. Popular lip products from American brands such as Summer Fridays, Fenty Beauty and Rhode emphasize glossy, fuller-looking lips. South Korean brands including Rom&nd and Peripera are known among teenagers and young adults for softer, gradient-style finishes.

Similar differences affect foundations, blushes and other product categories. Olive Young therefore cannot assume that every product popular in South Korea will automatically connect with U.S. shoppers.

At the same time, adapting too aggressively could create another problem. A limited American selection may weaken Olive Young’s appeal among Asian American customers who already know the retailer and expect access to its distinctive Korean offerings.

A stronger strategy would combine K-beauty’s signature strengths—including skincare-focused formulas, ingredient transparency and product innovation—with products designed for a broader range of American consumers.

Foundation shades are one clear opportunity. Korean brand Clio has gained attention among U.S. shoppers after expanding its products to serve more skin tones. Olive Young could follow that approach by offering products suited to Black, Latina, white and Asian American customers while highlighting innovations that remain difficult to find at mainstream beauty retailers.

Some shoppers are especially interested in products developed for features that American retailers may overlook.

“Even though I’m not too familiar with K-beauty, I’ve struggled with finding the right lash clusters or eyelash curler that fits my eyeshape,” senior Divya Isukapalli said. “I would be really interested in finding what’s right for me through Olive Young, especially if they provide products different from Sephora, which is where I usually shop.”

Los Angeles, home to the country’s largest Korean population, offered Olive Young a strategic starting point. Future locations in areas with sizable Asian American communities—including New York City, San Francisco and the Washington, D.C., Maryland and Virginia region—could help the retailer establish a loyal base before pursuing wider expansion.

That gradual approach could also make Korean beauty brands and trends more familiar to consumers in communities such as McLean as K-beauty moves further into the mainstream.

Olive Young’s central challenge is not persuading Americans to abandon their existing preferences. Its success will depend on demonstrating how K-beauty can meet needs that established retailers have yet to address.
