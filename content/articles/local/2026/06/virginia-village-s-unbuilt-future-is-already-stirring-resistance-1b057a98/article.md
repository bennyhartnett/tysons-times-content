As a resident of a city-owned apartment building in Virginia Village, I have watched the debate over whether Falls Church should reinvest in the existing community, redevelop the site or pursue a solution somewhere between those options.

Yet some opposition appears directed at a proposal that has not even been created. Studying what might be possible is not the same as approving a plan.

Virginia Village occupies 4.5 acres near Big Chimney Park, Bowl America and Winter Hill. Its 20 four-unit buildings provide homes for 80 families. The city owns nine of those buildings, while 11 remain privately owned, and officials have reserved money for additional acquisitions.

With the buildings nearing 80 years old, examining the property’s future is a civic responsibility—not an immediate threat. Gathering information now will allow residents and city leaders to weigh preservation, redevelopment and other possibilities before making any commitments.

The most promising part of this effort is the opportunity for the community to help shape what comes next. Residents and neighbors have joined workshops, discussions and feedback sessions, and their comments have been recorded and presented back to them. The process has not been flawless, but the engagement has been sincere at a time when public trust can be fragile.

Virginia Village contains some of Falls Church’s few naturally affordable homes. Affordable housing should not depend on the luck of finding one of those limited opportunities. The city should investigate how to preserve—and potentially increase—the supply.

These conversations have also helped community members hear perspectives beyond their own. Disagreement will remain, but an honest discussion about housing, neighborhoods and the future can still produce greater understanding.

Not everyone who needs affordable housing can attend every public meeting. Some people work multiple jobs, care for children or aging relatives, or are simply occupied with meeting everyday needs. Empty seats should never be interpreted as proof that the need is absent.

Anxiety about Virginia Village’s future is understandable. Housing is inseparable from stability, belonging and the meaning of home. But uncertainty makes careful public discussion more necessary, not less.

The future of the property will arrive whether Falls Church prepares for it or not. Preserving affordable housing and community will require residents, neighbors and city leaders to confront the choices together.
