Fairfax County supervisors are divided over a proposal that would prevent new businesses from selling cigarettes, vapes and certain other products within 1,000 feet of schools and child-care facilities.

The Board of Supervisors asked county staff in December to examine possible restrictions on tobacco, nicotine and hemp sales. The review followed a Virginia law adopted last year that allows local governments to regulate sales by new businesses near schools and child-care sites.

When staff presented recommendations to the board’s land use policy committee on Tuesday, March 11, supervisors offered sharply different reactions. Some viewed the plan as excessive, while others wanted to explore broader restrictions. One supervisor questioned whether the county could afford another enforcement responsibility during a difficult budget period.

Sully District Supervisor Kathy Smith, who chairs the committee, opposed the initiative.

“I am not in favor of doing this,” Smith said, citing the resources that enforcement would require. Although she acknowledged her position might be in the minority, she said the county must exercise caution when adding new responsibilities.

Providence District Supervisor Dalia Palchik expressed a willingness to continue considering the issue, saying, “I’m open to having conversations.”

## Proposed restrictions would cover many retailers

Staff recommended prohibiting sales of tobacco, nicotine and hemp products within 1,000 feet of the main entrances to public, private and parochial schools, as well as child-care facilities not operated from homes.

The rule would cover dedicated tobacco and vape shops along with supermarkets, convenience stores and gas stations where those products represent only part of overall sales.

Businesses that were already selling the covered products before the state law took effect on July 1, 2024, could continue doing so. Alexandria became the first locality in Northern Virginia to adopt such a ban last fall.

Brandie Temple, an epidemiologist with the Fairfax County Health Department, told supervisors that having retailers nearby “increases youth experimentation.”

Health Department estimates show that, at the end of 2024, approximately 40,920 young people in Fairfax County attended educational facilities located within 1,000 feet of a tobacco retailer. About 22% of the county’s existing retailers were within that distance.

## Supervisors debate where to draw the line

Board Chair Jeff McKay said the recommendations included worthwhile ideas but also provisions that could amount to “overreach.” He questioned including day-care facilities, calling that a step “too far.” McKay appeared more receptive to restrictions near elementary schools, though he remained skeptical about parts of the plan.

Mount Vernon District Supervisor Dan Storck pushed in the opposite direction, asking whether the county could also establish buffers around recreation and community centers and health-care and mental-health facilities.

County Zoning Administrator Leslie Johnson said she would review what Virginia law permits and return with an answer.

Braddock District Supervisor James Walkinshaw focused on police compliance checks showing that underage participants successfully purchased tobacco products in 23% of attempts.

“Clearly, all you have to do is go to four places … and one will sell [to] you,” Walkinshaw said. “It’ll take 30 minutes.”

McKay and Dranesville District Supervisor Jimmy Bierman requested more information about staffing needs and the financial consequences of adopting the restrictions.

“There’s got to be a cost-benefit analysis,” Bierman said.

McKay also called for code enforcement employees to participate in future discussions so supervisors could better understand how the proposal would work in practice. He noted that their jobs are already difficult.

Virginia lawmakers voted in 2019 to raise the minimum age for tobacco purchases from 18 to 21, while leaving the age at 18 for active-duty military personnel. Congress later prohibited tobacco sales nationwide to anyone younger than 21, and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration adopted final regulations for that federal restriction in 2024.
