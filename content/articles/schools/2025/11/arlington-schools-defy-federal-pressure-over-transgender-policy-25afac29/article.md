A packed Arlington School Board meeting became a heated debate over transgender students’ rights as district leaders vowed to maintain their inclusive policies despite the threat of losing federal funding.

Thirty-three people signed up to speak as the new school year approached. Alongside routine announcements about school excellence awards and “popsicles on the playground,” parents, students, educators and political figures debated whether transgender students should be treated according to their gender identity in school facilities.

Most speakers supported keeping the current Arlington Public Schools policy. Superintendent Dr. Francisco Durán rejected the Department of Education’s claim that the district was violating Title IX, the federal law prohibiting sex discrimination in federally funded education programs.

“Contrary to the Department’s assertion that APS is in violation of Title IX, our policy is consistent with both state and federal law, including the Virginia Values Act, and it is also supported by recent court decisions affirming protections for students,” Durán said.

Durán and School Board Chair Bethany Zecher Sutton both emphasized the district’s commitment to every student’s well-being.

Several parents criticized what they described as unprecedented federal interference in local education decisions. Students spoke about the importance of the district’s inclusive policy, while one intersex speaker discussed being born with sex characteristics that do not fit typical male or female definitions.

School Board candidate Monique “Moe” Bryant argued that protections involving Title IX were promises of dignity, equity, safety and opportunity rather than political tools. She also recalled that her father grew up in Arlington during segregation and knew what happened when communities failed to defend equality.

Wakefield High School teacher Magdalena Parkhurst said APS policies give students room to exercise individual liberties protected by the Constitution. She compared arguments over restroom access for transgender students with past efforts to segregate public facilities and claims that integration would endanger white women.

Republican Lt. Gov. Winsome Earle-Sears, a candidate for governor, sharply opposed the district’s approach. She called the policies dangerous and argued that male and female students should have separate sports teams, locker rooms and bathrooms.

Earle-Sears accused APS of damaging trust with parents and said the district was among five Virginia school systems violating Title IX. She warned that refusing to change the policy could put millions of dollars in federal education funding at risk.

Earle-Sears arrived with students and parents, some carrying signs supporting her campaign. Ashley Hendricks, who held a “safety for girls” sign, argued that allowing transgender girls to compete against cisgender girls could undermine athletic opportunities women fought to secure.

Hendricks invoked Kathrine Switzer, the first woman to officially run the Boston Marathon, while calling for separate and fair competitive spaces for girls. She said every athlete deserves an opportunity to participate but maintained that girls also deserve protected competition.

Earle-Sears, Hendricks and the other supporters who arrived with them left together after the lieutenant governor spoke.

Arlington is one of five Northern Virginia school systems directed by the Department of Education to end policies protecting transgender and nonbinary students. Earle-Sears appeared at Arlington’s board meeting on Aug. 21 and Loudoun’s on Sept. 9. She also appeared outside meetings in Prince William on Sept. 3, Fairfax County on Sept. 26 and Falls Church on Oct. 3.
