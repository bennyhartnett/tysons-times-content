Plans to open Western High School at the former King Abdullah Academy campus could face a crucial delay as Fairfax County officials decide whether the $150 million project requires a full public land-use review.

The Fairfax County Planning Commission was expected to consider the issue in January 2026. Its decision could determine whether Fairfax County Public Schools can welcome students as planned in fall 2026 or must undergo a process that could add months to the schedule.

At the center of the dispute is a review required under Virginia Code § 15.2-2232, commonly called the “2232 Process.” It determines whether a proposed public facility substantially aligns with the county’s Comprehensive Plan based on its general location, character and extent. The review does not evaluate construction quality or building-code compliance.

FCPS is seeking administrative approval under a designation known as “Feature Shown.” The school system argues that a full public hearing is unnecessary because the property has already operated as a school.

The Planning Commission must decide whether converting the campus into a public high school represents such a significant change that a full review is warranted. The debate follows earlier controversy surrounding the purchase and complaints about limited transparency and public input.

Supporters say the accelerated approach is necessary because high schools in western Fairfax County are severely overcrowded. At the Nov. 13, 2025, School Board meeting, Braddock representative Rachna Sizemore Heizer called the acquisition “a gift” that would help relieve that crowding.

The purchase has also been presented as potentially saving taxpayers $280 million compared with constructing an entirely new high school.

## School Board Pushes Ahead

The School Board voted 7-3-1 on Nov. 13 to approve a phased, opt-in enrollment plan for the 2026-2027 academic year.

That vote came after a Nov. 2 FCPS bulletin in which Hunter Mill representative Melanie K. Meren said the superintendent had acknowledged that assigning the first group of students for fall 2026 was too ambitious.

Franconia representative Marcia St. John-Cunning argued that the purchased campus should not remain unused.

“The community wants this. The students want this. We bought this school for a reason. We don't need it sitting there,” she said.

Meren and Mason representative Ricardy Anderson voted against the accelerated plan. They contend that operating a comprehensive public high school at the site would bring new attendance boundaries, heavier traffic and additional service demands that should be examined through a public process.

A full 2232 review would require FCPS to address anticipated effects involving transportation and vehicle trips, noise, environmental conditions, tree protection, landscaping and screening.

## Traffic and Programming Raise Questions

Traffic has emerged as one of the most contentious issues. Anderson has warned that principals could be left to manage a substantial increase in vehicles once the property becomes a public high school.

The opt-in enrollment plan could add to those concerns. Students living outside the school’s eventual attendance boundary would have to provide their own transportation, potentially increasing the number of private vehicles traveling to the campus. A full review would likely require a traffic-impact analysis.

Anderson also questioned whether the board had defined the school’s intended character and scope, noting that its special academic programming had not yet been selected. The board has described Western High as a comprehensive high school that will include a specialized program pathway.

Timing remains the central obstacle. Meren said even a best-case 2232 review could take five months, leaving insufficient time afterward to make necessary structural changes before fall 2026.

If the Planning Commission accepts the “Feature Shown” approach, FCPS would clear its most significant land-use hurdle without a full public hearing, reducing potential costs and delays.

If commissioners reject that approach, the school system would have to pursue the complete public-review process. The resulting delay could put the planned fall 2026 opening beyond reach.
