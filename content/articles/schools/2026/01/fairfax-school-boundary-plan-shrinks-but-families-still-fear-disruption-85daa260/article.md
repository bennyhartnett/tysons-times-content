Fairfax County Public Schools’ first comprehensive boundary review in 40 years could move 1,697 students this fall, prompting sharply divided reactions from families concerned about academic programs, friendships, transportation costs and divided communities.

Superintendent Michelle Reid’s recommended plan affects fewer students than the original proposal, which would have reassigned 2,210. The school board is scheduled to vote Thursday after another public hearing.

Nearly 100 speakers addressed the board during a Jan. 10 hearing. Some argued that boundary changes are overdue or should go further, while others warned that moving students could fracture support networks and disrupt their education.

Glasgow Middle School sixth grader James Sinclair learned of the initial proposal shortly before winter break. He worried it could send his brother, who struggles to make friends, to a different school.

Odessa Jansen, a rising senior at Justice High School, faced the possibility that her home would be reassigned to Falls Church High. She feared the move could interrupt her pursuit of an International Baccalaureate diploma because, she said, Falls Church does not offer IB courses.

Jansen said boundary changes became a constant subject among students, including during classes and lunch. They also created group chats to discuss the proposal, while some of her friends worried about access to particular sports.

Reid’s latest recommendation reversed the potential changes affecting Sinclair and Jansen. Their families believe advocacy may have helped produce the late revision.

Parent Kris Griffin welcomed the change, saying it spared her family from scrambling to relocate a junior already enrolled in an IB program to a school without the same curriculum.

## Herndon families seek broader changes

The recommended plan would send some students to Herndon Elementary School, but several Herndon-area parents said additional adjustments could improve efficiency and reduce transportation expenses.

Melissa Morrison, a PTA committee member at Herndon High School, described the review as worthwhile but questioned why some neighborhoods about 2½ miles from the school remain assigned elsewhere. Transporting those students costs the division millions of dollars, she said.

Nicole Meade, president of the Herndon Middle School PTA, addressed concerns that reassignment could affect property values. She said her own home in the Reston-Herndon area has not lost value in 10 years.

Other speakers focused on the social consequences of dividing established groups. A Rolling Valley Elementary fifth grader said the proposed changes would reduce—but not eliminate—a split feeder pattern, leaving even fewer classmates to continue together into middle and high school.

One high school student, speaking before the final recommendation was released, urged officials to remember that boundary decisions involve more than points on a map. The student warned that reassignment during crucial academic years could separate young people from friends, support systems and communities built over several years.

Cathy Hosek argued that boundary decisions should be considered alongside school programming. Each school pyramid needs a consistent foundation of opportunities, she said, because program availability influences capacity, staffing and transportation.

Parent Cheryl Schoenberg also rejected shifting small groups of students among schools as a lasting answer to overcrowding. Splitting communities to move a few students at a time, she said, does not provide a genuine solution.
