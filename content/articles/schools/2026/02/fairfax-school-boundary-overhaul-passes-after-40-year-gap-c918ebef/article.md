The Fairfax County School Board approved its first comprehensive boundary review in nearly four decades on Jan. 22, launching a recurring redistricting process while making immediate changes that affect less than 1% of the school division’s students.

The measure passed 8-3 after a two-year process marked by community meetings, procedural confusion and disagreements over whether the modest initial changes justified their cost. Superintendent Michelle Reid called the review a “great beginning” toward long-term progress, acknowledging that the process was imperfect but necessary.

The district-wide reassessment—the first since the mid-1980s—was designed to respond to changing demographics and uneven use of facilities across Virginia’s largest school system. During the intervening decades, the board generally made isolated boundary adjustments, including changes around Westfield High School when it opened in 2000 to ease severe overcrowding at Chantilly and Centreville high schools.

Several sites remain flagged for further study. New recommendations are expected by January 2027.

## An 8-3 Vote With One Seat Vacant

Eleven of the board’s 12 members participated. The Braddock District seat was vacant pending a March 3 special election.

Karl Frisch, Kyle McDaniel, Melanie K. Meren, Marcia St. John-Cunning, Robyn Lady, Seema Dixit, Ricardy Anderson and Board Chair Sandy Anderson supported the plan.

Ryan McElveen, Mateo Dunne and Ilryong Moon voted against it.

Supporters described the decision as hard-won groundwork for improving equity, balancing enrollment and using existing schools more efficiently. Opponents said last-minute revisions left both board members and the public uncertain about what the final motion would accomplish.

Dunne said he could not support a plan whose transportation details remained unclear, including which students would receive bus service and how much it would cost.

He also argued that the results were limited. Fairfax County Public Schools began the process with 22 attendance islands and ended with the same number. Split feeders, which send students from one elementary school to different high schools, declined from 42 to 35. The district’s approximately 800 trailers and modular classrooms were reduced by only one or two.

Dunne described the process as mentally exhausting and said it sometimes felt like “flying by the seat of your pants.”

McElveen characterized the outcome as an “incremental change at extraordinary cost,” arguing that targeted studies could have produced similar results. He also warned that repeated boundary reviews could consume future boards’ attention and criticized delays to school start-time changes during a student mental health crisis. He called the board’s approach an “unmitigated failure.”

Moon likewise objected to delaying start-time changes. He and McElveen also raised equity concerns about students allowed to remain at their current schools under grandfathering provisions without a finalized transportation plan. Dunne said families were being asked to trust that the district could manage the transition’s logistics.

## Supporters Call It Essential Groundwork

Lady said the plan reflected community input and would move students into schools with available enrollment capacity. She expressed pride and excitement in supporting it.

McDaniel said the changes would help align boundaries, balance enrollment, expand access and improve the operation of the large school division.

Meren voted for the plan but shared concerns about the process. She said some recommendations were not released publicly until shortly before the vote and emphasized that complete transparency was necessary for informed decisions.

The board began laying the groundwork in 2024 by updating Policy 8130 before changing any maps. The policy requires the superintendent to conduct a division-wide boundary review every five years, making regular reassessment a permanent part of the district’s planning.

Between October 2024 and November 2025, the board held multiple work sessions and community meetings focused partly on attendance islands and split feeders.

## Westfield Shows the Cost of Delayed Boundary Changes

Westfield High School illustrates how Fairfax County previously relied on construction to manage crowding.

The school opened in 2000 with capacity for 2,500 students after nearby boundaries were adjusted to relieve Chantilly and Centreville high schools. The district did not conduct a comprehensive boundary review at the time.

Westfield reached capacity by 2003. That November, voters approved a bond that included $8.7 million for a permanent 24-room addition, and construction began in 2004. The addition opened for the 2006-07 school year, but crowding continued.

Opening-day enrollment reached 3,230 students. Then-principal Tim Thomas joked that the school would soon have its own ZIP code. About 13 trailers surrounded the school during much of the 2010s, and facility reports showed 13 still on the site in January 2026.

Another capacity project is now underway at Western High School, the former Saudi Academy property purchased by the School Board for $150 million. The school is scheduled to begin limited, phased enrollment in August 2026 for the 2026-27 academic year.

Through the comprehensive review and the five-year cycle established by Policy 8130, the district is placing greater emphasis on shifting boundaries and balancing enrollment across existing buildings. The goal is to prevent overcrowding needs from overtaking the renovation schedule.

FCPS staff are also developing a new renovation queue to succeed the district’s 2008 list. The FY 2027-2031 Capital Improvement Program contains the status of schools in that queue, while separate bond records identify renovation projects approved by voters but still awaiting funding.

The FY 2026-2030 adopted capital plan lists Centreville High School for 2028 and includes Franklin Middle School and several elementary schools, among them Poplar Tree, Cub Run and Union Mill, ahead of any potential new placement for Westfield.
