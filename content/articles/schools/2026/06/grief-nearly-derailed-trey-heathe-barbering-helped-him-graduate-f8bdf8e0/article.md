Gold clippers glide across a client’s hair as Mountain View High School senior Trey Heathe carefully shapes the cut. Around campus, his talent is no secret. He rents a chair at a local salon, promotes his work on social media and operates a business website.

But building that reputation required Heathe to push through his mother’s death, homelessness, poverty and the exhaustion that nearly kept him from earning his diploma.

Heathe began cutting hair in seventh grade while living in New York City with his mother and brother. After his mother suffered a stroke, he needed to cut his own hair and began practicing with clippers.

“I practiced on myself for about a year,” he said. His mother became his first teacher and, after seeing his progress, eventually trusted him to cut her hair.

Heathe’s life changed again when she died during his high school years. He and his brother moved to Virginia, where they faced financial hardship, homelessness and the grief of losing their only parent.

After attending one high school, Heathe transferred to Mountain View expecting to enter his senior year. Instead, he was classified as a junior. The prospect of spending an additional year in high school deepened his fatigue.

Barbering became his escape. He filled his schedule with haircuts and sometimes worked until 3 a.m. to keep his mind occupied.

“That was my way of drowning out the reality,” Heathe said.

The pressure and burnout made regular school attendance difficult. As his absences mounted, his chance of graduating was put at risk.

At the same time, his barbering business was gaining momentum. Classmates recommended him to others, helping his customer base grow. Heathe also realized that opening his own shop would require a barbering license—and finishing high school was essential to reaching that goal.

“You can’t own your own shop without a license,” he said. “The only way to get there was to finish high school.”

Heathe traces his turnaround to Feb. 26, when he decided he would return.

“I told myself, ‘I am going to school tomorrow and ask them to enroll me again,’” he recalled.

Once back in class, he approached school with renewed discipline and organized his haircut appointments to keep his workload manageable.

History and social studies teacher Mike Hardy played a major role in Heathe’s return. Heathe said Hardy’s calm, steady and respectful way of dealing with people reminded him of his mother.

“I noticed that you never really lose your cool,” Heathe told him. “And that’s what my mom would do.”

That example also shaped how Heathe views barbering. He sees it as a “people business” in which managing emotions and treating customers respectfully matter alongside technical skill.

“I look up to how you do it,” he told Hardy. “You master it on a level where I’m trying to get there.”

Hardy, in turn, described Heathe as an “old soul” whose resilience and presence strengthen the classroom. He told Heathe that his commitment to showing up honored his mother, his first teacher, and inspired him as an educator.

Heathe continues to draw motivation from his mother, whom he remembers as strong and independent. He wears a pendant in her memory and revisits old text messages that remind him of the future she believed he could build.

“Everything she had faith in me about, I have to get it,” he said.

Heathe recently opened an account for his LLC and is exploring the steps required to earn his barbering license. Ahead of graduation, he has also volunteered to cut classmates’ hair so they can look their best at the ceremony.

As he prepares to receive his diploma, Heathe is urging others to look beyond their immediate circumstances.

“You can’t be comfortable with the now,” he said. “Start thinking about your future.”
