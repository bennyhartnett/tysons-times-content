A Muslim civil rights organization sued Fairfax County Public Schools on June 4, alleging several Thomas Jefferson High School students were unfairly suspended after filming a staged “kidnapping” video to promote their Muslim Student Association chapter.

The CAIR Legal Defense Fund filed the complaint in U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia in Alexandria. It argues that FCPS punished the Muslim students while taking no action against other student groups that produced similar videos, violating the students’ First and Fourteenth Amendment rights and federal civil rights laws.

According to the lawsuit, the video was based on a viral comedy format used by groups nationwide and was meant as a playful overreaction to students declining an invitation to attend a club meeting.

The students recorded the skit during lunch in the prayer room at Thomas Jefferson High School for Science and Technology in Lincolnia. One wore a Palestine sweatshirt, another wore clothing featuring a crescent moon and star with a keffiyeh, and two wore hijabs.

In the video, a student identified in the complaint as “Jane Doe” asks two others whether they plan to attend an MSA meeting. When they decline, students identified as “Richard Roe” and “John Doe” approach them from behind. One student is placed in a large plastic bin and covered, while another’s head is covered before both are moved behind a room divider.

Jane Doe then asks “Colin Coe” and another MSA member whether they will attend. They agree, and the video concludes with a message saying nobody was harmed during filming.

Two days after the video was posted to the MSA chapter’s Instagram account, it began circulating on X alongside comments connecting it to Hamas’ Oct. 7, 2023, attack on Israel. School administrators called the students to the front office the following day as part of an investigation.

The complaint says the students showed administrators examples of other FCPS organizations using similar promotional skits. Eight students were placed in detention the next day, and the plaintiffs did not return to their usual routines for more than a week, according to the filing.

At a June 4 press conference, the students’ parents described their children as leaders, athletes, honor students and volunteers. They said other groups at the same school had made comparable videos—including some depicting weapons—without being punished.

FCPS said it remained committed to every student’s safety and well-being but had not received the lawsuit. The district said its attorneys would respond through the legal process after reviewing the complaint and that disciplinary cases are handled individually under its Student Rights and Responsibilities protocols.

The dispute follows months of scrutiny over FCPS’ handling of alleged antisemitism. Last October, the Jewish Community Relations Council of Greater Washington criticized the MSA video, saying it was unacceptable to make light of violence during a war.

In late November, the U.S. House Committee on Education and the Workforce opened an investigation into FCPS and two other school districts over their responses to antisemitism. A letter from Chairman Tim Walberg listed concerns that included MSA chapters allegedly reenacting the Oct. 7 kidnappings in promotional videos. The supporting citation referenced the Thomas Jefferson video and a similar production by Langley High School students in McLean.
